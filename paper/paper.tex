\begin{abstract}
A motor learner can be helped by a model of its own body, learned from
self-generated movement, or by a more skilled teacher. Two questions about such
help are rarely tested with controls: whether a body model helps because of
what it has learned or merely because it adds imagined experience, and whether
a learner whose teacher is withdrawn is left dependent on it. This paper
answers both with controls that change one thing at a time. One soft
actor-critic learner is trained on six muscle-driven bodies, an elbow and a
finger from MyoSuite and four planar arms of one to four joints. A frozen body
model learned from reward-free babbling is compared with a random model in
identical imagination machinery; a frozen teacher is withdrawn, and compared
with replacing it by a frozen copy of the student, which keeps the imitation
objective and removes only the teacher. Every claim holds under two endpoints.
What the body model has learned matters more the more joints the body has: the
gap between the trained and the random model grows from 5~mm on one joint to
357~mm on four. On the finger, a learner whose competent teacher is withdrawn
keeps at least 60\% of the teacher's benefit. The sharp drop at withdrawal is
lost teacher knowledge when the teacher is withdrawn early, and the abrupt
change of objective when it is withdrawn later, after the student has absorbed
the teacher.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Developmental robotics, internal models, guidance, musculoskeletal control,
reinforcement learning.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{M}{uscle-driven} bodies are hard to control: each joint is
pulled by several muscles, and many activation patterns produce the same
movement, so the number of degrees of freedom to coordinate grows quickly with
the number of joints \cite{bernstein1967}. Deep reinforcement learning on such
bodies is slow and is mostly model-free \cite{berg2023,chiappa2023,schumacher2023}.
Two kinds of help are available to a learner before task learning begins. One
is an internal forward model of the body, predicting the consequences of motor
commands \cite{wolpert1995,kawato1999} and learned from self-generated
movement \cite{dearden2005}; an artificial learner can use it to imagine
experience. The other is a more skilled other: a teacher policy whose actions
the learner imitates.

Both kinds of help raise a question that existing studies leave open. For the
body model, the question is \emph{why} it helps. Imagined rollouts add data
whatever the model contains, and an inaccurate model can make model-based
learning worse than model-free \cite{janner2019,voelcker2022}; whether a model
learned from babbling helps through what it has learned, and how that depends
on the body, has not been isolated. For the teacher, the question is what
happens when it is taken away. Motor-learning research holds that guidance can
leave a learner dependent on it \cite{salmoni1984,schmidt1992}, and
teacher--student pipelines in robotics cut a distillation term to make the
student independent \cite{schmitt2018,agarwal2022}. Cutting the term removes
the teacher's knowledge and changes the student's objective at the same
moment, so a collapse at weaning \cite{agarwal2022} cannot be attributed to
either.

Both questions can be answered with a control that changes one thing at a
time. A random model in the identical imagination machinery isolates what the
body model has learned. A frozen copy of the student, swapped in for the
teacher at withdrawal, keeps the imitation objective and removes only the
teacher; comparing it with a plain withdrawal separates lost knowledge from
the change of objective.

The study applies these controls to one soft actor-critic learner on six
muscle-driven bodies (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}). The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item evidence that what a babble-trained body model has learned matters more
the more joints the body has: the gap between the trained and a random model
grows by about half the model-free error per added joint, while a random model
in the same machinery becomes increasingly harmful (Sec.~\ref{sec:model});
\item evidence that a competent teacher can be withdrawn without a lasting
deficit: on the finger the withdrawn learner keeps at least 60\% of the
teacher's benefit, more the longer it was taught (Sec.~\ref{sec:withdraw});
\item a decomposition of the drop at withdrawal into lost teacher knowledge
and the change of objective, showing that the first dominates when the teacher
is withdrawn early and the second when it is withdrawn later
(Sec.~\ref{sec:dip}).
\end{itemize}
Every claim holds under two endpoints and survives a correction for multiple
comparisons. Code and data will be released on acceptance.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Body models.} Developmental robotics \cite{lungarella2003,asada2009,cangelosi2015}
treats motor babbling as a way to learn forward models of the self
\cite{dearden2005}, and intrinsic motivation as what drives it
\cite{oudeyer2007}; self-modelling \cite{bongard2006} and goal babbling
\cite{rolf2010} learn body models by directed exploration. In model-based
reinforcement learning, World Models \cite{ha2018} trains a model once from
random rollouts; the Dreamer series \cite{hafner2020,hafner2021,hafner2025} and
TD-MPC2 \cite{hansen2024} keep the model learning online; MBPO
\cite{janner2019} branches short rollouts from real states. Plan2Explore
\cite{sekar2020} and APV \cite{seo2022} pretrain a model without task reward;
offline methods \cite{kidambi2020,yu2020,yu2021} fit a model to logged,
rewarded data. Muscle world models are co-learned with the skill
\cite{feng2023,kim2025}, and frozen models learned from reward-free actuation
drive soft and muscle-driven bodies by planning or model-only learning
\cite{berdica2024,wu2024modex,zheng2025}. That model error can make
model-based learning worse than model-free, more so in higher dimension, is
known \cite{janner2019,voelcker2022}. These studies evaluate a model against
model-free learning; how much of its benefit is due to what it has learned,
measured against a model with no knowledge in the same machinery, and how this
scales with the body, is not reported.

\textbf{Guidance and its withdrawal.} The guidance hypothesis
\cite{salmoni1984,schmidt1989,schmidt1992} holds that frequent augmented
feedback guides performance during acquisition but can degrade learning;
high-frequency physical guidance gives the poorest retention in one test
\cite{winstein1994}, and reduced knowledge of results improves delayed
retention in another \cite{winstein1990}. The evidence is mixed: one
meta-analysis finds the degrading effect limited to laboratory tasks,
parameter learning and extensive practice \cite{marschall2007}, a later one
finds no reliable benefit of reduced feedback frequency \cite{mckay2022}, and
children given reduced feedback learn worse \cite{sullivan2008}. A recurring
lesson is that performance while guided, or just after guidance ends, is not
the same as what has been learned \cite{schmidt1992,soderstrom2015}. Human
studies corrupt or remove the information in a feedback channel while keeping
the channel \cite{buekers1992,kitago2013,ploettner2025}. In teacher--student
learning, a teacher that sees privileged state is distilled into a student on
the student's own rollouts \cite{chen2019lbc,lee2020,miki2022,kumar2021}, and
the distillation term is annealed or cut to make the student independent
\cite{schmitt2018,agarwal2022}. Supervised distillation uses the student itself
or a random network as the teacher to separate what the term does from what the
teacher knows \cite{yuan2020,sarnthein2023}; a term toward the learner's own
earlier policy stabilises fine-tuning \cite{li2023proto,luo2023,zhao2022}. What
is missing is a comparison of the withdrawn student with one never taught,
with the teacher's knowledge held apart from the change of objective.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Bodies, task and learner}
\texttt{myoElbowPose1D6M} is a one-joint elbow driven by six muscles; error is
a joint-angle error in milliradians (mrad). \texttt{myoFingerReachRandom-v0}
is a four-joint index finger driven by five muscles; error is a Euclidean
fingertip error in millimetres (mm). Both are used with MyoSuite dynamics
\cite{caggiano2022}, a reach reward, a fixed start pose and eight training
targets. Four planar arms of one, two, three and four revolute joints, driven
by two, four, eight and ten MuJoCo muscles (antagonist pairs, biarticular from
three joints on), reach a planar fingertip target; error is in mm. Joint count,
muscle count and biarticular muscles therefore change together along the arm
series. Episodes are 100 steps; evaluation is greedy on twenty held-out
targets disjoint from the training targets. One soft actor-critic (SAC)
\cite{haarnoja2018,haarnoja2018b} learner is used in every condition: two
hidden layers of 128 units, update-to-data ratio 2, batch 128, learning rate
$3\times10^{-4}$, automatic entropy temperature. Conditions differ only in what
is added. The reward, given on every step in every condition, is the only
feedback a learner without help receives.

\subsection{The body model and its control}
The body model is a forward model $f_\theta(s,a)\mapsto s'$, a two-layer
256-unit network trained once on reward-free Ornstein--Uhlenbeck muscle
activations ($10^5$ transitions on the elbow, $2\times10^5$ on the other
bodies), then frozen. It is used for Dyna \cite{sutton1991} rollouts of three
steps branched from 200 real states every 100 environment steps; the reward at
an imagined state is the environment's analytic reach reward, computed with the
simulator's forward kinematics. Each update draws 64 real and 64 imagined
transitions. Conditions: \texttt{prior} uses the trained model;
\texttt{randprior} uses a randomly initialised model in identical machinery,
so the two differ only in what the model has learned; \texttt{blank} is SAC
alone with 128 real transitions per update; \texttt{blank64} uses 64 real
transitions, each repeated once, the real-data content of a Dyna update without
a model.

\subsection{The teacher and its withdrawal}
The teacher is a frozen actor. The student's actor loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\pi = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SAC}}
 + \frac{c}{|\mathcal{A}|}\big\|\tanh\mu_{\mathrm{student}}(o) - \tanh\mu_{\mathrm{teacher}}(o)\big\|^2 ,
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
on replayed observations $o$, where $|\mathcal{A}|$ is the number of muscles;
$c=1$ while the teacher is attached. The imitation term acts on the student's
actions on every update, the analogue of concurrent physical guidance rather
than of knowledge of results. On the elbow the teacher (T1) is the terminal
actor of a model-free run of 30k rewarded steps on a seed outside the study.
On the finger, the corresponding actor barely moves (169.1~mm held-out against
172.3 for a zero-activation policy), so the finger teacher (T2) was chosen to
be competent: the terminal actor of the best of twelve 100k body-model runs
(35.6~mm; the twelve range from 35.6 to 85.3). One student shares its
initialisation seed with that run; excluding it changes no conclusion.

At step $t_w\in\{2000,3000,\dots,8000\}$ the teacher is withdrawn.
\texttt{none} is never guided (identical to \texttt{blank}),
\texttt{constant} keeps the teacher, \texttt{abrupt} sets $c=0$ at $t_w$, and
\texttt{selfanchor} replaces the teacher at $t_w$ by a frozen copy of the
student's own actor, with $c$ unchanged (the swap fires two environment steps
before $t_w$, where the weight is last written). The withdrawn learner is
compared with \texttt{none} to ask whether it is left worse off than a learner
never guided. The drop at withdrawal is split by the self-anchor:
$\text{selfanchor}-\text{constant}$ is losing the teacher with the objective
kept (the \emph{teacher} component), $\text{abrupt}-\text{selfanchor}$ is
losing the imitation term (the \emph{loss-term} component). The snapshot holds
what the student has absorbed by $t_w$, so the teacher component measures only
what the teacher still has to add.

\subsection{Endpoints and statistics}
Each run lasts 100{,}000 steps and is evaluated every 2{,}000 steps (every
1{,}000 in the withdrawal conditions). Every claim is reported under two
endpoints: the mean of the six evaluations at 90k--100k (the \emph{late
mean}) and the single evaluation at 100k. The late mean was adopted after an
internal review because single evaluations of one run move by several mrad or
mm between neighbouring checkpoints; to rule out that a conclusion depends on
this choice, only results that hold under both endpoints are claimed. The
\emph{drop} at withdrawal is the error at $t_w{+}1000$ minus the error at
$t_w$. The seed is the unit of analysis: twelve matched seeds per condition,
twenty-four on the three- and four-joint arms, paired contrasts, paired-$t$
95\% intervals; bold or $^*$ marks an interval excluding zero. A non-detected
contrast is not evidence of no difference. Retention of a teacher's benefit is
tested directly: the withdrawn learner keeps at least a fraction $r$ of the
teacher's benefit if the one-sided 95\% upper bound of
$(\text{abrupt}-\text{none}) - r\,(\text{constant}-\text{none})$, pooled over
$t_w$ within each seed, is below zero. The twelve primary tests (the
trained-versus-random gaps on the two- to four-joint arms, the joint-count
trend, and the finger withdrawal contrasts, each under both endpoints) all
remain significant after a Holm correction (largest corrected $p=0.043$).

\subsection{Implementation details}
Actor and critics are SiLU networks, the twin critics with layer
normalisation; $\gamma=0.99$, Polyak $\tau=0.005$, Adam, target entropy
$-0.5\,|\mathcal{A}|$, gradient norm clipped at 10, real replay 200{,}000,
imagined replay 400{,}000, 1{,}000 warm-up steps. Episode time-outs are stored
as terminal transitions; imagined transitions are never terminal. Babble data
use $\theta=0.15$, $\sigma=0.4$, activations clipped to $[-1,1]$ and the pose
re-randomised every 100 steps. A matched seed sets the network initialisation,
replay sampling, the training environment and the order of target draws; runs
are deterministic on CPU. The 1{,}056 runs behind the results take about
1{,}100 single-thread CPU-hours.

\subsection{What the body model has learned matters more the more joints there are}
\label{sec:model}
Table~\ref{tab:model} compares the trained body model with a random model in
identical machinery. The two differ only in what the model has learned, and
the gap grows with the number of joints (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}a): on the
planar arms it is \ci{-4.92}{-10.28}{+0.43}~mm on one joint (detected only at
the single 100k evaluation), \ci{-28.42}{-41.59}{-15.25} on two,
\ci{-132.89}{-183.14}{-82.64} on three and \ci{-357.20}{-406.71}{-307.69} on
four. As a fraction of each arm's model-free error the gap is 0.05, 0.81, 0.75
and 1.78, and its per-seed slope across joint count is
\ci{-0.54}{-0.69}{-0.39} of the model-free error per joint (single
evaluation: \ci{-0.51}{-0.70}{-0.32}). The trained model also beats the random
one on both MyoSuite bodies, by \ci{-25.78}{-34.71}{-16.84}~mrad on the elbow
and \ci{-50.11}{-64.54}{-35.68}~mm on the finger. The gap is still widening at
100k on the one-, three- and four-joint arms (by \ci{-60.47}{-101.83}{-19.11}~mm
on three joints over the last 20k steps), so the reported gaps are not
inflated by incomplete training.

Most of the growth comes from the random model. Imagined experience from a
model that knows nothing about the body is nearly harmless on one joint
(\ci{-4.24}{-6.93}{-1.54}~mm against \texttt{blank}, late mean only) and
increasingly harmful after: \ci{+22.02}{+9.81}{+34.23}~mm on two joints,
\ci{+97.37}{+41.17}{+153.56} on three and \ci{+262.20}{+198.84}{+325.57} on
four (Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}). With more degrees of freedom there are more
ways for imagined transitions to be wrong, and the learner depends more on the
model being right. Against model-free learning the trained model is ahead on
the finger and on the one-, two- and four-joint arms under both baselines and
both endpoints; on the elbow and the three-joint arm its advantage depends on
the endpoint or the baseline (Table~\ref{tab:model}). Part of its advantage
over \texttt{blank} on the harder arms is the smaller real batch of a Dyna
update, which helps there by itself; the comparison with the random model,
which shares that batch, is free of this effect.

attached. Withdrawn at $t_w=2000$, the learner loses mostly the teacher's
knowledge: the teacher component is \ci{+45.97}{+36.74}{+55.20}~mrad on the

the change of objective. On the finger the loss-term component exceeds the
teacher component at every $t_w$ from 4000, by 17.87 to 46.48~mm; on the elbow
the teacher component is no longer detected from $t_w=5000$ on, and the
loss-term component exceeds it at $t_w=6000$ and $8000$. The teacher component
falls as attachment lengthens because the self-anchor holds what the student
has already absorbed; this fall therefore reflects absorption by construction,
and the new information is the size of what remains: the drop that follows a
late withdrawal is a reaction to the objective changing, and a term toward the
student's own snapshot removes most of it.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{A body model is worth what it has learned.} Imagination by itself is
not what helps. With a model that knows nothing about the body, imagined
experience costs little on one joint and becomes destructive as the number of
joints grows; with a model learned from babbling, the same machinery helps.
The difference between the two is the value of what babbling taught the
model, and it grows with the degrees of freedom the learner has to
coordinate \cite{bernstein1967}. For a developing learner, this is an argument
that self-generated movement pays most on the bodies that are hardest to
control, and that a wrong body model on such a body is worse than none.

\textbf{Taking a teacher away.} In the human sense, the sharp drop after
withdrawal is the retention-test analogue, and it is real: guidance does leave
the learner unable to perform at the guided level immediately afterwards
\cite{winstein1994}. What it does not leave, after continued practice, is a
deficit in what has been learned \cite{schmidt1992,soderstrom2015}: on the
finger most of a competent teacher's benefit persists, and the withdrawn learner
is never detectably behind one never guided on either body. The
self-anchor shows when the drop reflects learning and when it reflects
performance: early, the student has not yet absorbed the teacher and loses its
knowledge; later, the student has absorbed it, and the drop is the objective
changing under it. The setting differs from the human studies: the teacher's
attachment duration is varied rather than feedback frequency, and reward is
given on every step in every condition. A practical consequence follows for
pipelines that wean a student off distillation \cite{schmitt2018,agarwal2022}:
a late withdrawal can be smoothed by anchoring the imitation term to the
student's own snapshot rather than cutting it.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limits}
The study is single-task reach in simulation, on deterministic, fully
observed bodies, which favour a frozen forward model, and the imagined reward
uses exact forward kinematics; partially observed bodies and a learned reward
are the natural next test. Along the arm series, joint count, muscle count and
biarticular muscles change together, so the scaling is with the body's
complexity as a whole rather than with joints alone. Each random control is one
initialisation per seed. The finger's competent teacher is the best of twelve
candidate runs; teachers of graded quality would map the benefit against
competence. Withdrawal is a single step at 2k--8k steps; gradual fading and
late withdrawal are left to future work, as are longer training on the harder
bodies and a physical robot.

\section{Conclusion}
Two kinds of help for a muscle-driven learner are tested with controls that
change one thing at a time. A body model learned from babbling helps through
what it has learned, and that matters more the more joints the body has: a
random model in the same machinery turns from harmless to destructive as joints
are added. A competent teacher can be taken away: on the finger the learner
keeps most of what it was taught, and the drop at withdrawal is lost knowledge
only when the teacher leaves early; later it is the change of objective.
