\begin{abstract}
Developing learners rarely start from nothing: they have a model of their own
body and the guidance of more skilled others, and both kinds of help can be
given to an artificial motor learner. Prior work measures such help while it is
present. What it leaves behind once withdrawn is unknown, because a withdrawal
removes the help's knowledge and changes the training objective at the same
moment. This paper separates the two. One soft actor-critic learner is trained
on six muscle-driven bodies, an elbow and a finger from MyoSuite and planar arms
of one to four joints, with a frozen body model learned from reward-free
babbling and used for imagined rollouts, or with a frozen teacher acting
through an imitation term, each against a random counterpart, with twelve or
twenty-four matched seeds. A control that replaces the teacher by a frozen copy
of the student keeps the objective and removes only the teacher. The body model
helps on all six bodies, from a tenth of the error on one joint to nearly half
on four, and a random model harms more the more joints there are. A teacher is
worth its competence. Withdrawn, neither kind of help leaves the learner
detectably worse than one that never had it. On the finger the student keeps
70\% of its teacher's benefit, and the drop at withdrawal is the change of
objective, not lost knowledge. The body model's benefit lasts mainly while its
imagination runs.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Developmental robotics, internal models, guidance, musculoskeletal control,
reinforcement learning.

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{M}{uscle-driven} bodies are hard to control: each joint is
pulled by several muscles, the muscles act with delay, and many activation
patterns produce the same movement. Deep reinforcement learning on such bodies
needs hundreds of millions of environment steps
\cite{berg2023,chiappa2023,schumacher2023}, while humans and animals acquire
new movements in a handful of trials. Computational sensorimotor theory points
to two kinds of help a biological learner brings to a task. One is an internal
forward model of the body, predicting the consequences of motor commands
\cite{wolpert1995,kawato1999} and learned from self-generated movement. The
other is a more skilled other whose guidance steers early attempts. Both are
\emph{priors} in the developmental sense, present before task learning begins,
and both are easy to give an artificial learner: a model learned from motor
babbling supplies imagined experience, and a teacher policy supplies an
imitation target.

Help is eventually taken away. A caregiver steps back, a distilled teacher is
discarded \cite{schmitt2018,agarwal2022}, and a learned model is switched off.
Motor-learning research holds that guidance can breed dependence
\cite{salmoni1984,schmidt1992}, and scaffolding theory judges a tutor by what
the learner does after the tutor fades \cite{wood1976}. Existing studies do not
settle what help leaves behind. Model-based and teacher--student methods are
evaluated while the help is present. When a distillation term is cut, the
student loses the teacher's knowledge and a term of its objective at the same
moment, so a collapse at weaning \cite{agarwal2022} cannot be attributed to
either. Stopping a body model's imagination likewise changes how much real
data each update sees.

The key observation of this paper is that a withdrawal has to be split into
what the help knew and how the help entered training. For a teacher, replacing
it at withdrawal by a frozen copy of the student keeps the imitation term but
carries no further knowledge. The difference between this \emph{self-anchor}
and a plain withdrawal prices the term; the difference between the self-anchor
and keeping the teacher prices the teacher's knowledge. For a body model, the
learner that never had it must train exactly as the withdrawn learner does
after withdrawal. With these controls, and a random counterpart for each kind
of help, the two kinds of help are compared on equal terms while they are
present and after they are gone.

The study applies these controls to one soft actor-critic learner on six
muscle-driven bodies: a one-joint elbow and a four-joint finger from MyoSuite
\cite{caggiano2022}, and four planar arms of one to four joints. Fig.~\ref{fig:withdraw}
shows the central result: a learner whose teacher is withdrawn drops sharply,
recovers, and ends no worse than one never guided, and on the finger far ahead
of it. The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a controlled comparison of a frozen babble-trained body model and a
frozen teacher, each against a random counterpart, showing that the body model
helps on all six bodies and helps more the more joints the body has, and that
a teacher is worth its competence (Sec.~\ref{sec:model}--\ref{sec:teacher});
\item evidence that withdrawing a teacher leaves no detectable dependence: the
withdrawn learner keeps 70\% of the teacher's benefit on the finger, and on the
elbow any deficit stays within a quarter of the teacher's worth after 200k
steps (Sec.~\ref{sec:withdraw});
\item a decomposition showing that the drop at withdrawal is the change of
objective rather than lost knowledge, and that a body model's benefit lasts
mainly while its imagination runs (Sec.~\ref{sec:withdraw}--\ref{sec:bmwithdraw}).
\end{itemize}
Code and data will be released on acceptance.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Guidance and dependence.} The guidance hypothesis
\cite{salmoni1984,schmidt1989,schmidt1992} holds that frequent augmented
feedback guides performance during acquisition but degrades learning.
High-frequency physical guidance gives the poorest retention in one test
\cite{winstein1994}, and reduced knowledge of results improves delayed
retention in another \cite{winstein1990}. The evidence is mixed: a
meta-analysis questions whether frequent feedback degrades learning at all
\cite{marschall2007}, a later one finds no reliable benefit of reduced feedback
frequency \cite{mckay2022}, and children given reduced feedback learn worse,
not better \cite{sullivan2008}. These studies vary how \emph{often} feedback is
given and test retention without further practice. The present study varies
how \emph{long} a teacher is attached and measures the learner after continued
practice; it borrows the question, whether guidance leaves a learner worse off
without it, and does not test the hypothesis itself. Human studies corrupt or
remove the information in a feedback channel while keeping the channel
\cite{buekers1992,kitago2013,ploettner2025}, and models of dependence on
robotic assistance place it in an effort or slacking term of the learner's own
cost \cite{emken2007,casadio2012}. None separates the information from the
channel at the moment guidance is withdrawn.

\textbf{Teacher--student learning.} Privileged learning distils a teacher that
sees state the deployed policy will not into a student trained on its own
rollouts \cite{chen2019lbc,lee2020,miki2022}; RMA \cite{kumar2021} regresses the
privileged latent instead. Distillation is either the student's entire
training or an auxiliary term annealed or cut to make the student independent
\cite{schmitt2018,agarwal2022}. Weaning can collapse the student, which Agarwal
et al.~\cite{agarwal2022} attribute, without a test, to the change of
objective. A term toward the learner's own earlier policy stabilises
fine-tuning \cite{li2023proto,luo2023}. Supervised distillation uses the
student itself or a random network as the teacher to separate what the term
does from what the teacher knows \cite{yuan2020,sarnthein2023}, and compares
stopping distillation mid-way only with continuing it \cite{cho2019}; in
language models a teacher-free signal matches a noisy teacher \cite{ding2026}.
A cloned policy fine-tuned by off-policy reinforcement often degrades at the
transition, because a fresh critic gives unreliable values outside the data
\cite{nair2020,uchendu2023,zhang2023}; a retained cloning term prevents this
\cite{zhao2022}. What is missing is a comparison of the withdrawn student with
one never taught, with the change of objective held apart.

\textbf{Body models.} Developmental robotics
\cite{lungarella2003,asada2009,cangelosi2015} treats motor babbling as a way to
learn forward models of the self \cite{dearden2005}, intrinsic motivation as
what drives it \cite{oudeyer2007}, and infant reaching as the paradigm case
\cite{thelen1993}. In model-based reinforcement learning, World Models
\cite{ha2018} trains a model once from random rollouts; the Dreamer series
\cite{hafner2020,hafner2021,hafner2025} and TD-MPC2 \cite{hansen2024} keep the
model learning online; MBPO \cite{janner2019} branches short rollouts from real
states. Self-modelling \cite{bongard2006} and goal babbling \cite{rolf2010}
learn body models by directed exploration; Plan2Explore \cite{sekar2020} and
APV \cite{seo2022} pretrain a model without task reward; offline methods
\cite{kidambi2020,yu2020,yu2021} fit a model to logged, rewarded data. Most
muscle reinforcement learning is model-free
\cite{berg2023,chiappa2023,schumacher2023,wochner2022}; muscle world models are
co-learned with the skill \cite{feng2023,kim2025}, and frozen models learned from
reward-free actuation drive soft and muscle-driven bodies by planning or
model-only learning \cite{berdica2024,wu2024modex,zheng2025}. An inaccurate
model makes model-based learning worse than model-free, more so in higher
dimension \cite{janner2019,voelcker2022}. These studies price a model while it
is in use; how its worth scales with the body, and what remains when it is
removed, are open.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Bodies, task and backbone}
\texttt{myoElbowPose1D6M} is a one-degree-of-freedom elbow driven by six
muscles; error is a joint-angle error in milliradians (mrad).
\texttt{myoFingerReachRandom-v0} is a four-joint index finger driven by five
muscles reaching a three-dimensional fingertip target; error is a Euclidean
fingertip error in millimetres (mm). Four planar arms of one, two, three and
four revolute joints, driven by two, four, eight and ten MuJoCo muscles
(antagonist pairs, biarticular from three joints on), reach a planar fingertip
target; error is in mm. Errors are never compared across bodies. Episodes are
100 steps from a fixed start pose; training draws from eight fixed targets;
evaluation is greedy on twenty disjoint held-out targets, so every number is a
generalisation measurement (on the one-joint bodies an interpolation test). One
soft actor-critic (SAC) \cite{haarnoja2018,haarnoja2018b} backbone is used in
every condition; conditions differ only in what is added.

\subsection{The two kinds of help}
The \emph{body model} is a forward model $f_\theta(s,a)\mapsto s'$ trained once
on task-agnostic, reward-free Ornstein--Uhlenbeck muscle activations ($10^5$
transitions on the elbow, $2\times10^5$ on the finger and on each planar arm),
then frozen, and used for short-horizon Dyna \cite{sutton1991} rollouts
branched from sampled real states. The reward at an imagined state is the
environment's analytic reach reward, computed with the simulator's forward
kinematics, so this route is scoped to a forward model \emph{plus} a known
reward and fingertip map. Each update draws 64 real and 64 imagined
transitions where model-free conditions draw 128 real.

The \emph{teacher} is a frozen actor. The student's actor loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\pi = q\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SAC}}
 + \frac{c}{|\mathcal{A}|}\big\|\tanh\mu_{\mathrm{student}}(o) - \tanh\mu_{\mathrm{teacher}}(o)\big\|^2 ,
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
on replayed observations $o$, where $|\mathcal{A}|$ is the number of muscles
and $c=1$ while the teacher is attached; the critic trains on reward in every
condition. In the \emph{additive} regime ($q=1$ always) the imitation term
supplements the student's own policy gradient; this is the teacher used
everywhere unless stated. In the \emph{replacing} regime ($q=0$ while attached,
$q=1$ from withdrawal) only the teacher moves the actor until withdrawal, as in
reward-free distillation \cite{lee2020,miki2022}.

Two teachers are used. T1 on each MyoSuite body is the terminal actor of a
model-free run of 30k rewarded steps on a seed outside the study's twelve; it
scores 27.2~mrad on the elbow's held-out targets, and 169.1~mm on the finger's,
where a zero-activation policy scores 172.3, so the finger T1 barely moves. T2
on the finger is chosen to be competent: it is the terminal actor of the best
of the twelve body-model runs of 100k steps (35.6~mm held-out; the twelve range
from 35.6 to 85.3, mean 54.4), and it is the teacher of every finger withdrawal
experiment. One of the twelve students shares its initialisation seed with the
run that produced T2; excluding it changes no finger-T2 conclusion.

\subsection{Conditions}
\textbf{Worth of the help.} \texttt{blank} is the backbone alone;
\texttt{prior} adds the body model; \texttt{coach} adds the teacher.
\texttt{randprior} and \texttt{randcoach} replace the model or the teacher by a
randomly initialised one in identical machinery (on the one- and four-joint
arms the random model carries the input normalisers of an earlier body model,
which differ from the final one's by under 1.5\%). \texttt{blank64} builds every
update from 64 distinct real transitions, each repeated once to fill the batch:
the real-data content of a Dyna update and no model (SAC at batch 64, up to the
independent action samples drawn for the repeated rows). \texttt{priorcoach}
carries both kinds of help.

\textbf{Taking the teacher away} at step $t_w$: \texttt{none} (never guided,
identical to \texttt{blank}); \texttt{constant} (teacher kept, identical to
\texttt{coach}); \texttt{abrupt} ($c\to0$ at $t_w$); \texttt{selfanchor} (the
teacher is replaced by a frozen copy of the student's own actor, $c$
unchanged); \texttt{randanchor} (replaced by a freshly initialised actor); and,
in the replacing regime only, \texttt{gateon} ($q\to1$ at $t_w$ with the teacher
kept). The anchor swaps fire two environment steps before $t_w$, at the step
the teacher's weight is last written. The withdrawn learner is compared with
two references: with \texttt{none}, to ask whether it is worse off than a
learner never guided, and with \texttt{constant}, to ask what it gives up
against keeping the teacher. The self-anchor splits the second:
$\text{selfanchor}-\text{constant}$ is losing the teacher with the imitation
term kept (the \emph{teacher} component), $\text{abrupt}-\text{selfanchor}$ is
losing the term (the \emph{loss-term} component). The snapshot holds whatever
of the teacher the student has absorbed by $t_w$, so the teacher component
measures only what the teacher still has to add. Since the snapshot's gradient
is zero at the swap, the loss-term component is the value of keeping a
stabilising term after withdrawal, not an instantaneous shock.

\textbf{Taking the body model away} at step 8000: \texttt{keep} (identical to
\texttt{prior}); \texttt{purge} stops imagination and empties the imagined
buffer, after which every batch is 128 real, as in \texttt{blank};
\texttt{matched} withdraws as \texttt{purge} does but draws 64 real transitions
and repeats them, as in \texttt{blank64}. Each withdrawn condition is compared
with the never-had condition that trains as it does after withdrawal:
\texttt{purge} with \texttt{blank}, \texttt{matched} with \texttt{blank64}.

\subsection{Metrics and statistics}
\emph{Endpoint}: the mean of a run's six held-out evaluations at 90k, 92k,
\dots, 100k (the \emph{late mean}); single evaluations of one run move by
several mrad or mm between neighbouring checkpoints. The late mean replaced a
single 100k evaluation after an internal review. Under the single evaluation,
21 of the endpoint contrasts reported here change detection, among them the
elbow body model's benefit over \texttt{blank} and the elbow shortfall after
the model is withdrawn (neither detected at 100k alone). The pooled
withdrawn-versus-never-guided contrasts keep their detection, but the elbow's
one-sided bound on a deficit would be 10.76~mrad, above the teacher's worth,
so the non-inferiority statement on that body rests on the late mean and on the
longer runs of Sec.~\ref{sec:withdraw}. \emph{Dip}: error at $t_w{+}1000$ minus
error at $t_w$. \emph{Attachment trend}: per-seed ordinary-least-squares slope
of a contrast against $t_w$, per 1{,}000 attached steps.

The seed is the unit of analysis; every condition has $n=12$ matched seeds
except the three- and four-joint arms, which have $n=24$; every comparison is a
paired contrast on the seed; intervals are paired-$t$ 95\% intervals, and an
interval excluding zero is marked~$^*$ or set in bold. A non-detected contrast
is not evidence of no difference. Where a withdrawn learner is said not to be
worse off, the one-sided 95\% upper bound of its deficit is given and compared
with what the help was worth. Significant cells of Tables~\ref{tab:conv} and
\ref{tab:decomp} were checked with an exact Wilcoxon signed-rank test; the one
it does not confirm at $p<0.05$ is flagged~$\ddagger$. Convergence is checked
per condition as the paired change from the 70--80k window to the 90--100k
window: 28 of the 97 conditions behind the results are still improving or
worsening (4.9 expected by chance), while none of the four pooled
teacher-withdrawal contrasts of Table~\ref{tab:headline} is (elbow, additive:
\ci{+1.52}{-1.96}{+5.00}~mrad).

\subsection{Implementation details}
Actor and critics are two-layer 128-unit SiLU networks, the twin critics with
layer normalisation; $\gamma=0.99$, Polyak $\tau=0.005$, Adam at
$3\times10^{-4}$, target entropy $-0.5\,|\mathcal{A}|$, gradient norm clipped
at 10, real replay 200{,}000 transitions, imagined replay 400{,}000, 1{,}000
warm-up steps, update-to-data ratio 2, batch 128. Dyna: every 100 environment
steps, 200 start states are drawn from real replay and rolled $k=3$ steps under
the current actor through the frozen model. The forward model is a two-layer
256-unit SiLU network predicting the next state, trained with Adam at
$10^{-3}$ for 40 epochs on babble data ($\theta=0.15$, $\sigma=0.4$, clipped to
$[-1,1]$, pose re-randomised every 100 steps, no reward). Teachers have the
actor's architecture. Evaluation is one greedy 100-step episode on each of the
twenty held-out targets, every 2{,}000 steps, and every 1{,}000 in the
withdrawal conditions. A matched seed sets the network initialisation, replay
sampling, the training environment and the order of target draws; the targets
are fixed across seeds, and runs are deterministic on CPU. The body model sees
more transitions than T1 was trained on; rebuilding the model from exactly 30k
babble transitions changes its student's error at 12k by
\ci{+2.75}{-12.77}{+18.27}~mm on the finger and \ci{+1.55}{-0.87}{+3.97}~mrad
on the elbow, neither detected. The 1{,}608 runs behind the results take about
1{,}700 single-thread CPU-hours.

\subsection{The body model helps, more the more joints there are}
\label{sec:model}
The body model beats model-free learning on both MyoSuite bodies
(Table~\ref{tab:conv}, Fig.~\ref{fig:conv}): by \ci{-3.90}{-7.42}{-0.38}~mrad
on the elbow and \ci{-46.19}{-58.70}{-33.69}~mm on the finger. Part of a Dyna
update's effect could come from its smaller real batch alone, since smaller
batches can help off-policy learners by reducing critic overfitting
\cite{rybkin2025,nauman2024,obandoceron2023}; on the finger halving the real
transitions per update does help (\ci{-20.29}{-32.53}{-8.05}). Against this
\texttt{blank64} control the model still wins on both bodies, by
\ci{-25.90}{-33.77}{-18.04}~mm on the finger. The model's worth is its
content: a random model in the same machinery is worse than the trained one on
both bodies, and worse than no model on the elbow
(\ci{+21.88}{+14.60}{+29.15}).

The planar arms show how this worth scales with the body (Table~\ref{tab:arms},
Fig.~\ref{fig:arms}). The model beats model-free learning on every arm, by
10\%, 18\%, 20\% and 47\% of the model-free error on one to four joints; the
per-seed slope of this relative benefit is \ci{-0.10}{-0.19}{-0.01} per joint
(exact Wilcoxon $p=0.034$). The more joints there are to coordinate, the more
the imagined experience is worth. A random model runs the other way: harmless
or slightly helpful on one joint (\ci{-4.24}{-6.93}{-1.54}~mm, the one body
where it is not detectably worse than the trained model) and increasingly
harmful after, \ci{+262.20}{+198.84}{+325.57}~mm on four joints; its relative
harm grows by \ci{+0.44}{+0.30}{+0.58} of the model-free error per joint
($p<0.001$). Against \texttt{blank64} the trained model is ahead on the one-,
two- and four-joint arms and not detectably on the three-joint arm. Over the six
bodies the babble model beats model-free learning on six, \texttt{blank64} on
five and the random model on five.

\subsection{A teacher is worth its competence}
\label{sec:teacher}
On the elbow the teacher T1 leaves its student \ci{-8.65}{-12.25}{-5.05}~mrad
ahead of model-free learning and \ci{-4.75}{-6.28}{-3.22} ahead of the body
model. On the finger the teacher decides the outcome. T1, which barely moves,
holds its student \ci{+21.36}{+9.83}{+32.89}~mm \emph{above} model-free
learning, not detectably different from a student held near a random teacher.
T2 on the same body and seeds gives a student at 37.5~mm, near its teacher's
35.6: \ci{-61.71}{-74.98}{-48.44} below model-free learning and
\ci{-15.52}{-24.43}{-6.60} below the body model. Adding the body model to a
teacher changes little (Table~\ref{tab:conv}, last rows). Which kind of help is
worth more is therefore a property of the teacher, not of the route.

The teacher is withdrawn at every 1{,}000 steps from 2{,}000 to 8{,}000, and
each withdrawn learner trains on to 100k (Table~\ref{tab:headline},
Fig.~\ref{fig:withdraw}). On the finger the withdrawn learner ends
\ci{-43.25}{-54.72}{-31.79}~mm ahead of one never guided, pooled over the seven
withdrawal times: it keeps 70\% of the teacher's worth. It keeps more the longer
the teacher was attached, from \ci{-14.82}{-27.27}{-2.36} at $t_w=2000$ to
$-62.60$ at $t_w=8000$ (slope \ci{-8.70}{-10.07}{-7.33} per 1{,}000 attached
steps), and from $t_w=7000$ on it is not detectably behind a learner that kept
its teacher (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}, total). On the elbow the withdrawn learner
is not detectably different from one never guided at any $t_w$
(\ci{+1.76}{-1.36}{+4.89} pooled, no trend); any deficit is at most 4.31~mrad
at 95\% confidence, under half of the 8.65~mrad the teacher was worth. On this
body the teacher's advantage does not outlast it: the withdrawn learner is 9 to
12~mrad behind one that kept the teacher at every $t_w$.

Training twice as long tightens the elbow result. The conditions
\texttt{none}, \texttt{constant} and \texttt{abrupt} at all seven withdrawal
times run to 200k on the same seeds; their first 100k reproduce the runs above
at every checkpoint. Scored over 190--200k, the withdrawn learner is
\ci{+0.04}{-2.11}{+2.19}~mrad from one never guided, with a one-sided bound of
1.79~mrad against a teacher worth \ci{-7.50}{-10.18}{-4.82}; no single
withdrawal time is detected, and the gap to a learner that kept its teacher
persists (5.7 to 9.9~mrad, detected at every $t_w$). The never-guided learner
is still improving (\ci{-2.79}{-4.99}{-0.60} from the 170--180k window), and the
pooled contrast moves with it, from \ci{-2.32}{-5.25}{+0.61} at 170--180k to
level.

When the teacher \emph{replaces} the student's own policy gradient until
withdrawal, the outcome is the same. Withdrawn at 2k, 4k, 6k or 8k, the finger
learner ends \ci{-55.87}{-66.36}{-45.39}~mm ahead of one never guided (89\% of
the teacher's worth) and the elbow learner is not detectably different from it
(\ci{+1.09}{-2.64}{+4.82}). On the elbow the pure-imitation student is only
\ci{-4.01}{-6.57}{-1.44} ahead of model-free learning even with its teacher
kept, so this cell cannot bound a deficit below the teacher's worth. The
transition is harder in this regime: four thousand steps after withdrawal the
elbow learner is 80 to 202~mrad behind one that kept its teacher. A learner
that keeps its teacher but switches on its own policy gradient
(\texttt{gateon}) also falls, by 25 to 85~mrad, on average as far as one whose
teacher is replaced by its own snapshot. Much of the cost is therefore the
first use of the student's own gradient after an imitation-only phase, the
known collapse when a cloned policy is fine-tuned by off-policy reinforcement
\cite{nair2020,uchendu2023,zhang2023}. By the end of training the withdrawn
learner is not detectably different from a never-guided one.

\textbf{The drop at withdrawal is the change of objective.} Withdrawal is
followed by a sharp drop (Fig.~\ref{fig:withdraw}): at $t_w=8000$ the error
rises by \ci{+45.17}{+24.58}{+65.76}~mrad on the elbow and
\ci{+49.20}{+31.79}{+66.60}~mm on the finger over the next 1{,}000 steps,
relative to keeping the teacher (Table~\ref{tab:decomp}). The self-anchor,
which keeps the imitation term and removes only the teacher, removes most of
the drop: the teacher component is \ci{+4.95}{-1.24}{+11.15} on the elbow and
\ci{+2.97}{+0.24}{+5.70} on the finger, and the loss-term component is
\ci{+40.22}{+19.96}{+60.48} and \ci{+46.22}{+30.30}{+62.15}. A random anchor in
the same place is far worse than the self-anchor on both bodies, so the control
works because its target is where the student already points, not because any
term will do. Across the attachment sweep (Table~\ref{tab:sweep},
Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep}) the teacher component of the drop falls the longer the
teacher was attached (\ci{-6.23}{-7.79}{-4.68}~mrad and
\ci{-4.65}{-7.05}{-2.25}~mm per 1{,}000 steps): the longer the attachment, the
more of the teacher the student has absorbed, and the less the teacher still
has to add. The loss-term component does not fall; on the finger it grows
(\ci{+7.07}{+2.95}{+11.19}). The finger's drop against the never-guided learner
also grows with attachment (\ci{+9.34}{+2.07}{+16.61}), but most of that growth
is the never-guided learner's own progress over the same window, which changes
with $t_w$ (\ci{+6.92}{+2.34}{+11.50}). By the end of training the loss-term
component is not detected at $t_w=8000$ on either body; on the elbow what the
withdrawn learner gives up against a kept teacher is the teacher component,
\ci{+8.61}{+4.18}{+13.04}, the size of the teacher's worth.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{A lesson and a tool.} The two kinds of help leave different things
behind. A teacher's help is written into the policy: on the finger the student
that loses its teacher keeps most of what the teacher gave, more the longer it
was taught, and the self-anchor shows that what the teacher still has to add
at withdrawal shrinks as the student absorbs it. A body model's help is a way
of learning: it supplies imagined experience while it runs, is worth more the
more joints there are to coordinate, and is worth nothing, or harmful, when its
content is random. When imagination stops, part of what it bought is kept on
one of three bodies. On the elbow neither kind of help is worth much and
neither is kept, but neither leaves the learner worse off than having had no
help at all.

\textbf{Dependence.} No withdrawn learner, on either MyoSuite body and in
either regime, ends detectably behind one that was never guided, and where the
teacher is worth enough to bound the difference, the bound lies well below that
worth; on the elbow, training to 200k narrows it to a quarter of the teacher's
worth. The setting differs from the studies that motivate the concern:
attachment duration rather than feedback frequency, and assessment after long
continued practice rather than a no-practice retention test
\cite{winstein1990}. Human studies do find withdrawal costs at retention after
fixed practice \cite{winstein1994}, and a field study finds students whose
tutor was withdrawn below students who never had one \cite{bastani2025}. After
long practice a weaned learner can end above a from-scratch one
\cite{schmitt2018}, and a regulariser removed after the early phase costs
little \cite{golatkar2019}, though early experience can also leave lasting
marks on a deep reinforcement learner \cite{nikishin2022}. Here early guidance
leaves a head start rather than a handicap.

\textbf{Practical consequences.} The drop that follows withdrawal is the
objective changing under the learner, and a term toward the learner's own
snapshot removes it. Pipelines that wean a student off distillation
\cite{schmitt2018,agarwal2022} can therefore taper or re-anchor the term rather
than cut it, without giving up what the teacher taught. More generally, a
withdrawal that changes two things at once cannot be read as the cost of the
thing withdrawn: the teacher needs a self-anchor to separate its knowledge
from its loss term, an imitation-only learner a \texttt{gateon} condition to
separate the teacher from the first use of its own gradient, and the body
model a never-had reference with the same real data per update, without which
a smaller batch reads as a retained benefit.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limits}
The study is single-task reach in simulation, on deterministic, fully observed
bodies, which favour a frozen forward model; the one-joint bodies' held-out
targets interpolate between the training targets, and the imagined reward uses
exact forward kinematics. Partially observed, stochastic bodies and a learned
reward are the natural next test of the body model. The finger's competent
teacher is the best of twelve candidate runs; T1 shows that a teacher that
barely moves gives the opposite result, and a graded series of teachers would
map worth against competence. The self-anchor holds what the student has
absorbed by withdrawal, so the fall of the teacher component with attachment
measures absorption by construction; an anchor taken at a fixed earlier time
would test it independently. Withdrawal is a single step, attachment varies
from 2k to 8k steps, contrasts were not pre-registered, the endpoint was
changed after an internal review, and several conditions are still moving at
100k, among them the four-joint arm's withdrawn learner; only the elbow is
trained to 200k. Gradual fading, longer training on the harder bodies and a
physical robot are left to future work.

\section{Conclusion}
A body model and a teacher are given to a muscle-driven learner, controlled
with a random counterpart, and taken away. A frozen babble-trained body model
helps on all six bodies, more the more joints the body has, and its benefit
lasts mainly while its imagination runs. A teacher is worth its competence,
and once withdrawn it leaves no learner detectably worse than one never taught;
on the finger most of what it taught stays, and the drop at withdrawal is the
change of objective, not forgetting.
