\begin{abstract}
A motor learner can be helped by a more skilled teacher or by a model of its
own body learned from self-generated movement. Withdrawing a teacher removes
its knowledge and changes the learner's objective at once, so whether
withdrawal leaves the learner worse off, and why it drops, have not
been separated; for a body model, whether imagined
experience helps because of what the model has learned is rarely tested. This
paper uses controls that change one thing at a time, on one soft actor-critic
learner and six muscle-driven bodies: an elbow and a finger from MyoSuite and
four planar arms of one to four joints. A withdrawn teacher is compared with
replacing it by a frozen copy of the student, which keeps the imitation
objective and removes only the teacher; a frozen body model learned from
reward-free babbling is compared with a random model in identical imagination
machinery. On the finger, a learner whose competent teacher is withdrawn ends
ahead of one never guided, and the share of the teacher's benefit it keeps
rises with the time the teacher was attached, from about a quarter after 2,000
steps to about all of it after 7,000. There, after 4,000 or more steps
of attachment, most of the drop that follows withdrawal is the removed
imitation term rather than the teacher's not-yet-absorbed knowledge. On
planar arms, a random model, whose predictions barely depend on the
action, becomes increasingly harmful as joints are added; the babble-trained
model avoids this harm.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Developmental robotics, internal models, guidance, musculoskeletal control,
reinforcement learning.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{M}{uscle-driven} bodies are hard to control: each joint is
pulled by several muscles, and many activation patterns produce the same
movement, so redundancy grows quickly with the number of joints
\cite{bernstein1967}. Deep reinforcement learning on such bodies is slow and is
mostly model-free \cite{berg2023,chiappa2023,schumacher2023}. Two kinds of help
are available to a learner before task learning begins. One is a more skilled
other: a teacher policy whose actions the learner imitates. The other is an
internal forward model of the body, predicting the consequences of motor
commands \cite{wolpert1995,kawato1999} and learned from self-generated
movement \cite{dearden2005}; an artificial learner can use it to imagine
experience.

Both kinds of help raise a question that existing studies leave open. For the
teacher, the question is what happens when it is taken away. Motor-learning
research holds that guidance can leave a learner dependent on it
\cite{salmoni1984,schmidt1992}, and teacher--student pipelines in robotics cut
a distillation term to make the student independent
\cite{schmitt2018,agarwal2022}. Cutting the term removes the teacher's
knowledge and changes the student's objective at the same moment, so a
collapse at weaning \cite{agarwal2022} cannot be attributed to either. For the
body model, the question is \emph{why} it helps. Imagined rollouts add data
whatever the model contains, and an inaccurate model can make model-based
learning worse than model-free \cite{janner2019,voelcker2022}; how much of a
babble-trained model's effect is due to what it has learned, and how that
depends on the body, has not been isolated.

Both questions can be answered with a control that changes one thing at a
time. A frozen copy of the student, swapped in for the teacher at withdrawal,
keeps the imitation objective and removes only the teacher; comparing it with
a plain withdrawal separates lost knowledge from the change of objective. A
random model in the identical imagination machinery isolates what the body
model has learned.

The study applies these controls to one soft actor-critic learner on six
muscle-driven bodies (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}). The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item evidence that a competent teacher can be withdrawn without leaving the
learner behind one never guided: on the finger the share of the teacher's
benefit the withdrawn learner keeps rises with the time it was taught, and is
more than half on average over the withdrawal times tested
(Sec.~\ref{sec:withdraw});
\item a self-anchor control showing that on the finger, after 4,000 or more
steps of attachment, most of the drop that follows withdrawal is the removed
imitation term rather than the teacher's not-yet-absorbed knowledge
(Sec.~\ref{sec:dip});
\item evidence that a body model is worth its content: on a series of planar
arms, imagined experience from a random model, whose predictions barely depend
on the action, becomes increasingly harmful as joints are added, while the
babble-trained model avoids this harm (Sec.~\ref{sec:model}).
\end{itemize}
The ten primary tests hold under two endpoints and survive a Holm correction
(Sec.~\ref{sec:stats}). Code and data will be released on acceptance.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Guidance and its withdrawal.} The guidance hypothesis
\cite{salmoni1984,schmidt1989,schmidt1992} holds that frequent augmented
feedback guides performance during acquisition but can degrade learning;
high-frequency physical guidance gives the poorest retention in one test
\cite{winstein1994}, and reduced knowledge of results improves delayed
retention in another \cite{winstein1990}. The evidence is mixed: one
meta-analysis finds the degrading effect limited to laboratory tasks,
parameter learning and extensive practice \cite{marschall2007}, a later one
finds no reliable benefit of reduced feedback frequency \cite{mckay2022}, and
children given reduced feedback learn worse \cite{sullivan2008}. Performance
while guided is not the same as what has been learned
\cite{schmidt1992,soderstrom2015}. Human studies corrupt or remove the
information in a feedback channel while keeping the channel
\cite{buekers1992,kitago2013,ploettner2025}. In teacher--student learning, a
teacher that sees privileged state is distilled into a student on the
student's own rollouts \cite{chen2019lbc,lee2020,miki2022,kumar2021}, and the
distillation term is annealed or cut to make the student independent
\cite{schmitt2018,agarwal2022}. Supervised distillation uses the student itself
or a random network as the teacher to separate what the term does from what the
teacher knows \cite{yuan2020,sarnthein2023}; a term toward the learner's own
earlier policy stabilises fine-tuning \cite{li2023proto,luo2023,zhao2022}, and
a cloned policy fine-tuned by off-policy reinforcement often degrades when the
constraint is removed \cite{nair2020,uchendu2023,zhang2023}. What is missing is
a comparison of the withdrawn student with one never taught, with the teacher's
knowledge held apart from the change of objective.

\textbf{Body models.} Developmental robotics \cite{lungarella2003,asada2009,cangelosi2015}
treats motor babbling as a way to learn forward models of the self
\cite{dearden2005}, and intrinsic motivation as what drives it
\cite{oudeyer2007}; self-modelling \cite{bongard2006} and goal babbling
\cite{rolf2010} learn body models by directed exploration. In model-based
reinforcement learning, World Models \cite{ha2018} trains a model once from
random rollouts; the Dreamer series \cite{hafner2020,hafner2021,hafner2025} and
TD-MPC2 \cite{hansen2024} keep the model learning online; MBPO
\cite{janner2019} branches short rollouts from real states. Plan2Explore
\cite{sekar2020} and APV \cite{seo2022} pretrain a model without task reward;
offline methods \cite{kidambi2020,yu2020,yu2021} fit a model to logged,
rewarded data. Muscle world models are co-learned with the skill
\cite{feng2023,kim2025}, and frozen models learned from reward-free actuation
drive soft and muscle-driven bodies by planning or model-only learning
\cite{berdica2024,wu2024modex,zheng2025}. Model error can make model-based
learning worse than model-free, more so in higher dimension
\cite{janner2019,voelcker2022}. These studies evaluate a model against
model-free learning; a comparison with a model that has learned nothing, in the
same machinery and across bodies of increasing complexity, is not reported.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Bodies, task and learner}
\texttt{myoElbowPose1D6M} is a one-joint elbow driven by six muscles; error is
a joint-angle error in milliradians (mrad). \texttt{myoFingerReachRandom-v0}
is a four-joint index finger driven by five muscles; error is a Euclidean
fingertip error in millimetres (mm). Both are used with MyoSuite dynamics
\cite{caggiano2022}, a reach reward, a fixed start pose and eight training
targets. Four planar arms of one, two, three and four revolute joints, driven
by two, four, eight and ten MuJoCo muscles (antagonist pairs, biarticular from
three joints on), reach a planar fingertip target; error is in mm. Joint count,
muscle count and biarticular muscles therefore change together along the arm
series. On the one-joint arm the muscles turn the joint only to $\pm0.86$~rad
of the $\pm2$~rad target range, so 10 of its 20 held-out targets are out of
reach and its errors include a floor of about 57.8~mm; across arms only
differences between conditions are compared, never absolute errors. Episodes
are 100 steps; evaluation is greedy on twenty held-out targets disjoint from the
training targets. One soft actor-critic (SAC) \cite{haarnoja2018,haarnoja2018b}
learner is used in every condition: two hidden layers of 128 units,
update-to-data ratio 2, batch 128, learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, automatic
entropy temperature. Conditions differ only in what is added. The reward is
given on every step in every condition, and every evaluation is made without
any help.

\subsection{The teacher and its withdrawal}
The teacher is a frozen actor. The student's actor loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\pi = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SAC}}
 + \frac{c}{|\mathcal{A}|}\big\|\tanh\mu_{\mathrm{student}}(o) - \tanh\mu_{\mathrm{teacher}}(o)\big\|^2 ,
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
on replayed observations $o$, where $|\mathcal{A}|$ is the number of muscles;
$c=1$ while the teacher is attached. The imitation term is high-frequency
prescriptive feedback about the action, applied to replayed states; the teacher
never moves the student in the environment, so it is closer to knowledge of
performance than to physical guidance. On the elbow the teacher (T1) is the
terminal actor of a model-free run of 30k rewarded steps on a seed outside the
study. On the finger, the corresponding actor barely moves (169.1~mm held-out
against 172.3 for a zero-activation policy), so the finger teacher (T2) was
chosen to be competent: the terminal actor of the best of twelve 100k
body-model runs (35.6~mm; the twelve range from 35.6 to 85.3). One student
shares its initialisation seed with that run; excluding it changes no
conclusion.

At step $t_w\in\{2000,3000,\dots,8000\}$ the teacher is withdrawn.
\texttt{none} is never guided, \texttt{constant} keeps the teacher,
\texttt{abrupt} sets $c=0$ at $t_w$, and \texttt{selfanchor} replaces the
teacher at $t_w$ by a frozen copy of the student's own actor, with $c$
unchanged for the rest of training (the swap fires two environment steps
before $t_w$, where the weight is last written). The withdrawn learner is
compared with \texttt{none} to ask whether it is left worse off than a learner
never guided. The \emph{drop} at withdrawal is the rise in error over the
1{,}000 steps after $t_w$ relative to \texttt{constant}; the self-anchor splits
it: $\text{selfanchor}-\text{constant}$ is losing the teacher with the
objective kept (the \emph{teacher} component), $\text{abrupt}-\text{selfanchor}$
is losing the imitation term (the \emph{loss-term} component). On the elbow the
drop is read from a separate set of runs with the same seeds and code,
evaluated every 1{,}000 steps, which reproduce the 100k runs at their shared
checkpoints. The snapshot
holds what the student has absorbed by $t_w$, so the teacher component measures
only what the teacher still has to add, and it shrinks with attachment partly
by construction.

\subsection{The body model and its control}
The body model is a forward model $f_\theta(s,a)\mapsto s'$, a two-layer
256-unit network trained once on reward-free Ornstein--Uhlenbeck muscle
activations ($10^5$ transitions on the elbow, $2\times10^5$ on the other
bodies), then frozen. It is used for Dyna \cite{sutton1991} rollouts of three
steps branched from 200 real states every 100 environment steps; the reward at
an imagined state is the environment's analytic reach reward, computed with the
simulator's forward kinematics. Each update draws 64 real and 64 imagined
transitions. Conditions: \texttt{prior} uses the trained model;
\texttt{randprior} uses a randomly initialised network in identical machinery
(one random network per body, shared by all seeds, as the trained model is one
checkpoint per body), with the trained model's input and output normalisers (on the one- and
four-joint arms those of an earlier build, which differ by at most 0.02
standard deviations). Its predictions barely depend on the action: across the
four arms, a change of action moves its predicted joint state 2--4\% as much as
it moves the trained model's. \texttt{blank} is SAC alone with 128 real
transitions per update; \texttt{blank64} uses 64 real transitions, each
repeated once, the real-data content of a Dyna update without a model.

\subsection{Endpoints and statistics}
\label{sec:stats}
Each run lasts 100{,}000 steps and is evaluated every 2{,}000 steps (every
1{,}000 in the withdrawal conditions). Final performance is reported under two
endpoints: the mean of the six evaluations at 90k--100k (the \emph{late mean})
and the single evaluation at 100k. The late mean was adopted after an internal
review because single evaluations of one run move by several mrad or mm between
neighbouring checkpoints; claims about final performance are made only when
they hold under both. The seed is the unit of analysis: twelve matched seeds
per condition, twenty-four on the three- and four-joint arms, paired contrasts,
paired-$t$ 95\% intervals; bold or $^*$ marks an interval excluding zero. A
non-detected contrast is not evidence of no difference. The withdrawn learner
keeps at least a fraction $r$ of the teacher's benefit if the one-sided 95\%
upper bound of $(\text{abrupt}-\text{none}) - r\,(\text{constant}-\text{none})$,
pooled over $t_w$ within each seed, is below zero. The threshold $r=0.5$, a
conventional ``more than half'', was chosen during revision, after a
descriptive bound had been computed; the largest $r$ that passes is reported
as that descriptive bound.
Effects of joint count are tested between bodies, with each arm's seeds
independent (Welch $t$). The primary family, specified during revision, has
ten tests: on the finger, the
withdrawn-minus-never-guided contrast, retention of at least half the teacher's
benefit and the slope of the withdrawn learner's advantage over $t_w$; and the
trained-minus-random gap on the three- and four-joint arms against the
one-joint arm; each under both endpoints. All ten remain significant after a
Holm correction (largest corrected $p=0.0018$). The drop at withdrawal is
measured from single evaluations; its components are compared per $t_w$ with a
Holm correction across the seven $t_w$ of each body.

\subsection{Implementation details}
Actor and critics are SiLU networks, the twin critics with layer
normalisation; $\gamma=0.99$, Polyak $\tau=0.005$, Adam, target entropy
$-0.5\,|\mathcal{A}|$, gradient norm clipped at 10, real replay 200{,}000,
imagined replay 400{,}000, 1{,}000 warm-up steps. Episode time-outs are stored
as terminal transitions; imagined transitions are never terminal. Babble data
use $\theta=0.15$, $\sigma=0.4$, activations clipped to $[-1,1]$ and the pose
re-randomised every 100 steps. A matched seed sets the network initialisation,
replay sampling, the training environment and the order of target draws; runs
are deterministic on CPU. The 1{,}056 runs behind the results take about
1{,}100 single-thread CPU-hours.

On the finger, with a competent teacher, the withdrawn learner ends far ahead

\ci{-43.25}{-54.72}{-31.79}~mm pooled over the seven withdrawal times

advantage over the never-guided learner is \ci{-8.70}{-10.07}{-7.33}~mm per
1{,}000 attached steps (\ci{-8.44}{-9.89}{-6.99}). Withdrawn at 2,000 or 3,000
steps it keeps less than half (point estimates 24\% and 36\%); averaged over the seven withdrawal times it
keeps more than half (one-sided test against one half, $p=0.0008$; single
evaluation $p=0.0018$), and at one-sided 95\% confidence at least 62\% (60\%
at the single evaluation). From $t_w=7000$ on it is not
detectably behind a learner that kept its teacher, under either endpoint.

On the elbow the teacher is worth less, \ci{-8.65}{-12.25}{-5.05}~mrad, and its
benefit goes with it: the withdrawn learner ends 9.2 to 12.2~mrad behind one
that kept its teacher at every $t_w$ (7.6 to 17.0 at the single evaluation),
and is not detectably different from one never guided
(\ci{+1.76}{-1.36}{+4.89}; single evaluation \ci{+3.42}{-5.58}{+12.42}). With a
benefit this small, 100k steps cannot exclude a small deficit, so the elbow
conditions were run on to 200k on the same seeds (their first 100k reproduce
the runs above at every checkpoint). Over 190--200k the withdrawn learner is
\ci{+0.04}{-2.11}{+2.19}~mrad from one never guided (single 200k evaluation:
\ci{-0.51}{-5.20}{+4.18}); under both endpoints any deficit is smaller than the
teacher's full worth, though not smaller than half of it at the single
evaluation (one-sided 95\%). The never-guided learner is still improving at
200k, so this comparison is not made at a plateau.

harder bodies, but that, along the arm series, imagination whose content is
uninformative becomes increasingly costly, and the babble-trained model avoids
that cost. The MyoSuite bodies do not follow joint count: the random model
harms the one-joint elbow (\ci{+21.88}{+14.60}{+29.15}~mrad against
model-free learning) and not detectably the four-joint finger
(\ci{+3.92}{-14.49}{+22.34}~mm). The
gaps are still widening at 100k on the one-, three- and four-joint arms and
may be narrowing on two joints (\ci{+9.96}{-0.42}{+20.35} over the last 20k
steps).

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Taking a teacher away.} Every evaluation in this study is made without
the teacher, so the drop that follows withdrawal is not a retention-test
analogue: it is a decline during continued practice once the training signal
changes. On the elbow the teacher's benefit does not outlast it, but no deficit
relative to never being guided is detected. On the finger a competent
teacher's benefit largely persists, the more so the longer the teacher was
attached, and the withdrawn learner ends far ahead of one never guided. The
self-anchor shows what the drop consists of: on the finger, after longer
attachment, it is mostly the removed imitation term, and the teacher's
not-yet-absorbed knowledge accounts for less of it.
The setting differs from human studies: attachment duration rather than
feedback frequency is varied, and reward is given on every step in every
condition.

\textbf{A body model is worth its content.} On the planar arms, imagination
with a model that ignores the action costs little on one joint and much on
four; with a model learned from babbling, the same machinery helps. What
babbling buys is therefore, at least in part, protection against uninformative
imagination, and along the arm series that protection matters more as joints
are added. Against model-free learning with matched real data, however, the
trained model's own advantage is not shown to grow with joint count, so the
data do not show that a correct model is worth more on harder bodies.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limits}
The study is single-task reach in simulation, on deterministic, fully
observed bodies, which favour a frozen forward model, and the imagined reward
uses exact forward kinematics; partially observed bodies and a learned reward
are the natural next test. The real replay buffer holds the whole run, so the
benefit a withdrawn learner keeps includes guided-phase transitions that remain
in its buffer; a buffer purged at withdrawal would separate the two. Each body
has one teacher, and the finger's competent teacher is the best of twelve
candidate runs; teachers of graded quality would map the benefit against
competence. Along the arm series, joint count, muscle count and biarticular
muscles change together, and the one-joint arm cannot reach half of its
targets; each body has one trained and one random model, shared by all
seeds, so variation between model draws is not sampled, and a model that uses
the action but has learned the wrong body would be a stronger control.
Withdrawal is a single step at 2k--8k steps; gradual fading and withdrawal
after more than 8k steps are left to future work, as are longer training on the
harder bodies and a physical robot.

\section{Conclusion}
A teacher and a body model are given to a muscle-driven learner and tested
with controls that change one thing at a time. A competent teacher can be taken
away: on the finger the share of what the teacher gave that the withdrawn
learner keeps rises with the time it was taught, and after longer attachment
the drop that follows withdrawal is mostly the removed imitation term. A body
model learned from babbling is worth its content: on planar arms, a random
model in the same machinery becomes increasingly harmful as joints are added,
and the trained model avoids that harm.
